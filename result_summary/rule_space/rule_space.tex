\hrule
\vspace{0.45cm}

\colorbox{soft}{\parbox{0.96\textwidth}{
\textbf{The idea in one paragraph.} Every FOV becomes a row of numbers: one column per
rule, holding that rule's lift in that FOV, and a neutral value where the rule was not
found. PCA then places the FOVs so that FOVs with similar rules land near each other.
Nothing about the group an FOV belongs to enters the calculation. The colours are added
afterwards, which is what makes the question fair: if the groups come apart, the rules
did it on their own.
\hypertarget{sep-guide}{\textbf{How to read the separation number.}} Every PCA carries
one. For each FOV it compares the distance to FOVs of its own group against the distance
to the nearest other group. \textbf{0 means the groups are completely mixed; 1 means they
sit apart with clear space between them; below 0 means an FOV is typically nearer the
other group than its own.} Each group is averaged on its own first, and the groups are
then averaged evenly, so a large group cannot decide the answer by being dense.
\textbf{The per-group values follow in brackets, and they matter.} One tight group
surrounded by a scattered one gives a decent average while only the tight group scores
well --- a different picture from two groups genuinely sitting apart. The number exists so
two pictures can be ranked, which the eye cannot do reliably; it is printed on each plot
and in the panel titles.
\textbf{The settings.} Pairwise rules, lift as the value, attraction and avoidance both
kept, self-rules kept, and a rule must fire in more than 10\% of a scope's FOVs to be
used. That last one is the setting that matters most, and Section~1.11 shows why.
\textbf{The data.} MIBI images of gut biopsies after stem-cell transplant and of healthy
controls, one FOV per image, every cell typed and placed. The second control group
(\texttt{Control\_S}) is left out throughout.
}}

\section{What the method does, and what it does not}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{First: is there any structure at all?}

Start with every FOV of both organs in one PCA, coloured by organ. This is the easiest
test the rules could be given, because colon and duodenum are known to differ. They fail
it: separation is $+0.009$, which is no grouping at all, and neither organ scores on its
own (Colon $-0.04$, Duodenum $+0.06$). Cell composition manages $+0.114$ over the same
FOVs.

Do not read that as the method failing. Section~1.11 shows the organ signal is real but
lives in the rules this configuration deliberately throws away --- the rare ones. What
follows is about disease stage, where the opposite is true.

\evidence{Negative at this setting; see Section~1.11}

\onefig{pos_all_pca_organ}{%
One dot per FOV, placed by its rules and coloured by organ, using only the rules
where two cell types attract each other (lift above~1) --- so every column says how much
more often they are neighbours than chance would give. The grey lines under the title give
the colouring and the separation, then the settings the matrix was built with. Axis labels
give the share of all variation each component carries. Circled dots are FOVs drawn as
tissue below; the blue square marks the closest pair of FOVs from different groups. See the
\protect\hyperlink{sep-guide}{separation guide above}.}

\onefig{posneg_all_pca_organ}{The same FOVs with the avoidance rules kept as well.
Values follow the figure above; the settings line records which of the two this is.}
\label{fig:organ}

The components are thin, though less so than they look. PC1 and PC2 hold 15\% of the
variation across all FOVs and up to 24\% inside a stage pair --- roughly twice what the
unfiltered matrix gives, because dropping rare rules drops most of the neutral fill value
with them. Every flat picture below is still a slice of a rounder cloud.

\onefig{sum_variance}{%
One bar per scope, split into the share of all the variation PC1 carries (dark) and PC2

bar is the two together, then how many FOVs it was fitted on and how many rule columns
survived the 10\% threshold. Scopes are in the order the sections below take them.}
\label{fig:variance}

\subsection{Do the coordinates mean anything on the tissue?}

Before trusting any grouping, check that being close in this plot means being alike as
tissue. Take the two FOVs from different groups that land nearest each other and draw
them: they should look more like each other than like their own groups.

\evidence{Supportive}

\widefig{posneg_all_pair_fovs}{%

one patient are excluded, because two FOVs of one biopsy look alike whatever the method
does.}
\label{fig:pair}

\evidence{Supportive}

\subsection{Does the picture recover something we already know?}

The strongest test available without any statistics: take a fact about the tissue that is
established independently, and see whether the plot reproduces it while being shown no
labels at all. Healthy gut is known to look much the same from person to person, and
diseased gut is known to vary. If the rules capture anything real about how tissue is
organised, Control FOVs should sit closer together in this plot than disease FOVs.

They do. \textbf{Duodenal Control FOVs sit at 0.53 of the typical distance apart}, against
0.83 for Severe and 1.05 for Mild --- roughly half the spread of the disease groups.
Nothing in the calculation was told which FOVs were controls.

\evidence{Strong}

\onefig{posneg_duodenum_group_spread}{%
One bar per stage, duodenum. The bar is the mean distance between that stage's own FOVs
divided by the mean distance between any two FOVs in this plot, so \textbf{1.0 means that
stage's FOVs are no more alike than FOVs in general} and shorter is tighter. The dashed
line marks 1.0. The number on each bar is the value and how many FOVs it rests on.}
\label{fig:spread}

The colon does not reproduce it: Mild is tightest at 0.85, with Control at 0.93 and Severe
loosest at 1.05. Control is at least on the tight side here, but it is not the tightest
group, and the cohort is the obvious reason to be careful --- there are only four colonic
controls, and they are adjacent-normal tissue from patients with colon cancer rather than
healthy gut.

\evidence{Weak; four control biopsies}

\onefig{posneg_colon_group_spread}{Values follow Figure~\ref{fig:spread}, for the colon.}

Pooling the organs gives the cleanest version of the claim, and the only place in this
document where the three stages come out in order: Control 0.87, Mild 0.98, Severe 1.05.
Tissue gets less like itself as disease gets worse.

\evidence{Moderate}

\onefig{posneg_all_group_spread}{Values follow Figure~\ref{fig:spread}, both organs
together.}

\subsection{The hard question: is this just which cells are there?}

The test the whole investigation turns on. If counting the cells in each FOV places them
the same way, the rules are adding nothing and the arrangement of cells is not what we are
seeing. The answer is now close rather than one-sided: composition leads in five of seven
scopes, by margins of 0.04 rather than 0.09, and in the two duodenal scopes the two draw
level --- Mild versus Severe is $+0.181$ against $+0.177$, a gap of four thousandths that
is not worth calling a win either way.

Those scopes are worth a second look for a better reason than the total. The rules and the
cell counts pick out \emph{opposite} groups: in Duodenum Mild versus Severe the rules
define Severe (${+}0.31$ against Mild's ${+}0.06$) while the counts define Mild
(${+}0.29$ against Severe's ${+}0.06$). Two methods with the same overall score describing
different halves of one contrast is more interesting than either score, and it argues for
combining them rather than ranking them.

\evidence{Close; level in two scopes of seven, behind in five}

\subsection{Which rules were even findable?}

A sharper version of the same doubt. A rule can only be found in an FOV holding enough of
both its cell types, and that alone is a fact about composition. So build a third matrix
recording only whether each rule \emph{could} have fired, and give it the same PCA. It
knows nothing about where any cell sits.

\textbf{In the duodenal stage pair the lift values now win.} $+0.268$ against $+0.255$ for
rules-as-fired and $+0.252$ for could-have-fired: how strong a rule is carries more than
which rules exist, which is the first thing in this document to say the metric itself is
doing work. The colon goes the other way ($+0.223 / +0.278 / +0.307$), and by organ
could-have-fired still leads a very weak field ($+0.009 / +0.016 / +0.097$).

\evidence{Positive in the duodenum, negative in the colon}

\subsection{Holding composition fixed}

Everything above compares rules against composition while letting composition vary freely,
which loads the question. The fair test is to hold it fixed: split the FOVs into two
groups purely by what they are made of, take a group that still holds both labels, and fit
a fresh PCA on those FOVs alone. Their composition is alike, so anything that separates
them there is the rules' doing.

The result is split, and worth stating carefully. \textbf{Across organs it helps a great
deal}: the matched group of 128 FOVs gives $+0.154$, against $+0.009$ for the rules on all
FOVs and $+0.114$ for composition itself, with the duodenum at $+0.28$. So the organ
signal the rules could not find in Section~1.1 is partly recoverable once composition is
held still.

\textbf{Inside a stage pair it does not help.} Colonic Control-versus-Severe drops from
$+0.223$ to $+0.173$ and duodenal from $+0.268$ to $+0.124$. Both matched groups are much
smaller (40 and 37 FOVs), and the duodenal one is badly unbalanced at 29 Control against 8
Severe, which is exactly the shape that produces a one-sided answer --- and does
(Control $+0.46$, Severe $-0.22$). The honest reading is that composition was contributing
to the stage separations, and that these matched groups are too small and too skewed to
say by how much.

\subsection{What is at the edges of the cloud?}

Are the extremes real tissue types or artefacts? Eight directions are swept around the
middle and the FOV furthest out in each is drawn, so the shape of the cloud decides how

Every number above depends on one choice --- how often a rule must fire to be kept. Sweeping
it is the most informative thing in this investigation, because \textbf{organ and stage want
opposite answers}.

\evidence{Strong}

\section{Where this agrees with the atlas}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Healthy tissue is the same from person to person}

The clearest agreement is the result already shown in Figure~\ref{fig:spread}, where it was
used as evidence that the method works. It is worth naming again here as agreement, because
the fact it recovers comes from the atlas and not from this analysis.
\paperquote{Normal duodenum was stereotypical across individuals} The atlas makes that
claim specifically about the duodenum, and the duodenum is exactly where rule space
reproduces it --- Control FOVs at 0.53 of the typical distance apart, against 0.83 and 1.05
for the two disease groups. Pooling the organs orders the three stages correctly.

\evidence{Strong in the duodenum; see Figure~\ref{fig:spread}}

\subsection{The disease is patchy, but a patient is still a patient}

\paperquote{aGVHD is a patchy disease} The atlas finds heterogeneity inside a single
patient large enough to sometimes span the whole cohort. Rule space partly agrees. Most
patients are internally consistent --- 58 of 71 have FOVs closer together than average,
median 0.67 --- but a minority are as spread out as the cohort itself. Both halves matter:
the consistency says the rules are reading the patient, and that is also a warning, since
FOVs from one patient are not independent observations.

\evidence{Moderate; agrees in part}

\widefig{posneg_all_patient_spread}{%
One dot per patient over all FOVs of both organs. Across is that patient's own FOV spread
divided by the spread of any two FOVs, as in Figure~\ref{fig:spread}, so left of 1.0 means

numbers so patients sharing a count do not stack into one line.}

\subsection{The atlas's own analysis is the right-hand column}

Worth stating plainly, because it reframes Section~1. The atlas summarises its cohort with
a PCA over cell composition and phenotype, and that PCA separates control from mild and
severe disease. That analysis is the right-hand panel of Figure~\ref{fig:panel} --- the
baseline the rules are measured against. So the comparison is not rules against nothing; it
is rules against the published method, on the same FOVs.

\subsection{What does not carry over}

The atlas reports that immune composition tracks the time since transplantation, with
dynamics shared across individuals. Rule space does not recover it. Colouring the same PCAs
by days after transplant, donor type, corticosteroid response and survival gives separation
between $-0.01$ and $-0.17$ in every scope --- no grouping at all. These colourings are drawn
in the notebook; none is reproduced here, because there is nothing in them to see.

\evidence{Clear negative}

\section{Three observations worth following up}
% =============================================================================

None is in the atlas, and none is strong enough to stand alone. All three come out of
figures already shown.

\textbf{Rare rules and common rules answer different questions.} The threshold table is the
clearest result in this document: the organ split needs rules that fire in a few percent of
FOVs, and every stage split needs rules that fire in a fifth of them. If that holds, tissue
identity and disease state are written at different frequencies in the same matrix, and no
single threshold can read both.

\textbf{Rules and cell counts see opposite halves of the same contrast.} In Duodenum Mild
versus Severe the rules define the Severe group ($+0.31$) and barely see Mild ($+0.06$),
while the cell counts do the reverse ($+0.06$ and $+0.29$). Two methods with almost the same
overall score describing different things is more interesting than either score, and it
suggests the two should be combined rather than ranked.

\textbf{Avoidance rules matter in the duodenum and not in the colon.} Adding rules where
cell types avoid each other takes duodenal Control-versus-Severe from $+0.187$ to $+0.268$
and Mild-versus-Severe from $+0.075$ to $+0.181$; the colon moves the other way, $+0.234$
down to $+0.223$. If it holds, the two organs reorganise differently under disease --- the
duodenum by cell types moving apart, the colon by cell types coming together.

% =============================================================================
\section*{Where this leaves the method}
% =============================================================================

Five things are established. The coordinates mean something on the tissue: near FOVs look
alike and PC1 tracks a visible gradient. The rules recover a known biological fact without
being shown any labels --- healthy duodenum is by far the tightest group in the plot, at
0.53 of the typical spread, and pooled across organs the three stages come out in order.
Both groups of a stage pair now score on their own, so the separations are two groups
sitting apart rather than one tight group among scattered FOVs. In the duodenal stage pair
the lift values beat both the fired and the could-have-fired matrices, which says the
strength of a rule carries more than its presence. And with composition held fixed across
organs, the rules reach $+0.154$ where they managed $+0.009$ on all FOVs.

Three are not. Cell composition still leads in five scopes of seven and draws level in the
other two, though now by about half the margin it had. Holding composition fixed inside a stage pair lowers the separation rather
than raising it, on groups of 37 and 40 FOVs that are too small and too skewed to conclude
from. And no number here has been checked against chance: the next step is to shuffle the
labels a few hundred times and see what separation appears when there is nothing real to
find. Until that is run, treat every value above as a comparison between figures rather
than as a result on its own.

The most likely reading overall: the rules do carry information about arrangement, and this
configuration shows more of it than the last one did --- but an unsupervised PCA over the
whole rule matrix mixes composition and arrangement in one place, and composition is the
louder of the two. The threshold table points at the way forward more clearly than any
single figure: different questions live at different rule frequencies, and reading them
needs more than one matrix.
