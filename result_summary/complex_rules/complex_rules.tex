\section*{How to read this report}
% =============================================================================

Stated once here, so no caption repeats it.

\textbf{What a rule is.}
\begin{itemize}\tightlist
\item A \emph{pair rule} links two cell types: where A is, B tends to be.
\item This report uses only \emph{three-cell rules}, in two shapes: \textbf{A + B $\rightarrow$ C} and
\textbf{A $\rightarrow$ B + C}.
\item Each rule is measured inside one field of view (FOV) of one biopsy.
\item \emph{Attractions} pull cells together. \emph{Avoidances} push them apart.
\item A rule is \textbf{new} when no pair inside it was found in that same field.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Which trends count as a finding.} Only the \textbf{control-to-severe} gap. A rule that jumps in
mild disease and returns to its control level by severe is not treated as a result here, however large
the jump. Those rules stay in the notebooks; the few mentioned in this report are flagged as such.

\textbf{The five numbers} above every tissue panel. A neighbourhood is the cells within a fixed radius of
one cell; $L$ and $R$ are the rule's two sides; probabilities are shares of the field's neighbourhoods.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{llp{7.6cm}}
\toprule
\emph{supp} & $P(L \wedge R)$ & share of neighbourhoods holding the whole pattern \\
\emph{conf} & $P(L \wedge R)\,/\,P(L)$ & how often $R$ appears when $L$ does \\
\emph{lift} & $P(L \wedge R)\,/\,(P(L)\,P(R))$ & 1 = chance; 2 = twice chance; below 1 = keeping apart \\
\emph{lev}  & $P(L \wedge R) - P(L)\,P(R)$ & plain excess over chance; negative = keeping apart \\
\emph{conv} & $(1-P(R))\,/\,(1-\mathit{conf})$ & how strongly chance would break the rule \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}\tightlist
\item \emph{FDR} is the rule's permutation p-value after Benjamini--Hochberg. Only FDR $\leq0.05$ is kept.
\item Lift hits exactly 0 at total separation. So avoidance strength is \emph{inverse lift}, and leverage
is quoted whenever lift reaches 0.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The six verdicts.} Each longer rule is compared with the best pair inside it, same field:
\begin{itemize}\tightlist
\item \emph{a shorter rule said it}, \emph{consequents do it alone} --- redundant.
\item \emph{shorter rules too weak}, \emph{backing pair too weak} --- cannot be judged.
\item \emph{clearly stronger} --- its lift beats that pair by a set margin.
\item \emph{nothing shorter mined} --- no pair found in that field at all. This is a \textbf{new} rule.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Two denominators.} ``All FOVs'' counts every field. ``Eligible FOVs'' counts only fields holding
$\geq20$ cells of every type the rule names --- so a rule cannot look lost merely because its cells are.

\textbf{Staging.} Everything is staged by \textbf{clinical score} unless stated.
Section~\ref{sec:staging} runs it again under the pathological score; where they disagree is itself a
result.

\textbf{The seven figure types, and how every number in them is produced.}

\textbf{1. Verdict bars.} Per rule shape: count rules carrying each verdict, divide by rules of that
shape. Number inside a block = that percentage. Count at right = rules of that shape.

\textbf{2. Gain ranking.} For one rule, over every field where it holds at FDR $\leq0.05$:
\begin{itemize}\tightlist
\item \emph{strength} = median lift (for avoidance, median of $1/\mathrm{lift}$).
\item \emph{parent} = median strength of the strongest pair that is both inside the rule and itself mined
in that same field.
\item \emph{gain} = strength $\div$ parent.
\item No pair mined anywhere? Parent becomes the mining threshold (lift 1.2 attract, inverse lift 1.25
avoid), so gain is a floor, written ``$\geq$''.
\item Open circle = parent, filled = strength, ``FOVs'' = distinct fields. Rules in $\geq5$ fields, top 15.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3. Prevalence heatmap.} Numerator = fields of that stage where the rule holds at FDR $\leq0.05$;
denominator = fields of that stage in the chosen denominator. Both printed. Ranked by (largest stage
share $-$ smallest), keeping rules with $\geq5$ rule-bearing fields in one stage; top 12.

\textbf{4. Prevalence, strength and abundance} --- three panels:
\begin{itemize}\tightlist
\item \textbf{Left:} the heatmap fraction, for the rule and its strongest pair.
\item \textbf{Middle:} fields carrying the rule only. Faint dot = one patient's median lift over their own
such fields. Diamond = median of those. Patient counts read ``rule $|$ pair''.
\item \textbf{Right:} every field of the stage --- each rule cell type's share of that field's cells, one
faint dot per field, line through stage medians.
\end{itemize}
The right panel is the control for the left. A rule can only be said to have lost an \emph{arrangement}
if its cells are still there to be arranged.

\textbf{5. Availability bars.} Every field in exactly one state. Grey: some rule cell type has $<20$
cells. Otherwise teal (rule holds), beige (only a pair holds), pale (neither).

\textbf{6. Tissue panels.} Cells at measured positions. Panel 1 = all cells by type; panel 2 = only the
rule's types, rest grey; panel 3 = same for its best pair. Where a stage has no field with the rule but
one with a pair, that field is shown and the rule row is marked ``not found''. When two of a rule's own
cell types would look alike, one is recoloured for that figure only.

\textbf{7. Single-field proofs.} One field: everything, then the rule, then its best pair.

\textbf{What is and is not claimed.}
\begin{itemize}\tightlist
\item Each field result is a real statistical test.
\item Stage percentages and patient medians are descriptive, not patient-level tests.
\item Cells near each other does not prove one causes the other.
\item Purple = verbatim atlas quotes. Green \textbf{Biology} = our interpretation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Does a longer rule ever say something new?}
% =============================================================================

A three-cell rule might be a pair rule in disguise. So each is compared with the best pair inside it, and
gets one of three answers: it repeats a shorter rule, it beats one, or it has no shorter rule at all.

Nothing in this section depends on staging.

\subsection{Most long rules are redundant --- and the method says so}

Before any statistical filter, most long rules are already labelled as restatements of something shorter.
That is the honest baseline: the method is not claiming every longer rule is useful.

\fig{verdicts_attracts_all.png}{All mined attraction rules, before any statistical
filter.}{fig:v-att-all}

Keeping only rules that survive the statistical test clears the unjudgeable band away, and what remains
splits very unevenly between the two ways of being informative.

\fig{verdicts_attracts_supported.png}{Attraction rules at FDR $\leq0.05$.}{fig:v-att-sig}

\note{What this means for the rest of the report.}{Longer rules earn their place by finding what pairs
missed --- 29\% with no shorter rule, against 6\% that beat a measured pair.}

Avoidance splits the opposite way, which is worth seeing because it says the two rule types are
informative for different reasons.

\fig{verdicts_avoids_all.png}{All mined avoidance rules.}{fig:v-avo-all}

\fig{verdicts_avoids_supported.png}{Avoidance rules at FDR $\leq0.05$: 40\% clearly stronger, 5\%
new.}{fig:v-avo-sig}

\bio{Attraction rules find contexts nothing shorter saw; avoidance rules sharpen separations the pair
already hinted at --- what you expect if avoidance mostly reports tissue compartments.}

The same split computed inside each stage, and then inside each organ, comes out nearly identical --- so
none of Part 1 depends on how patients are staged or which gut region is looked at.

\fig{verdicts_attracts_by_stage_clin.png}{Attraction verdicts, clinical stages.}{fig:v-att-stage}

\fig{verdicts_attracts_by_stage_path.png}{The same split under pathological stages.}{fig:v-att-stage-path}

\subsection{When a longer rule beats a real pair, the gain is small}

Here are the biggest \emph{measured} gains in the whole screen --- and the interesting thing is how
modest they are. Not one reaches 1.6$\times$.

\fig{gain_attracts_measured.png}{Attraction rules with a measured pair to beat, top 15 by gain, in
$\geq5$ fields.}{fig:gain-dumbbell}

\bio{Adding a third cell type to an existing pair buys 30--50\% more lift at best --- the honest ceiling
on ``longer rules are stronger''.}

Letting in rules that have no pair to compare against changes the ranking completely: they take over the
entire list, and their gains are several times larger.

\fig{gain_attracts_with_new.png}{The same ranking, including rules with no mined pair. Their gains are
floors, written ``$\geq$''.}{fig:gain-dumbbell-new}

\subsection{Two fields that show the two ways of winning}

This is a strength win in a single field: Epithelial and Muscle together predict Paneth cells at lift
3.77, where Epithelial alone manages only 1.89.

\fig[0.92\textwidth]{attracts_fov_stronger.png}{A strength win in one duodenal
field.}{fig:proof-strength}

\bio{Epithelium is everywhere in this field and cannot locate Paneth cells; adding Muscle names the
crypt-base compartment, which is where they belong.}

This is the other kind of win, and the more useful one. Adding Epithelial barely moves lift at all, but
it takes the rule from statistically meaningless to solid.

\fig[0.92\textwidth]{attracts_fov_significance.png}{A significance win: lift 1.29 $\rightarrow$ 1.25, but

\bio{Plasma near CD4 T cells is not a finding --- both are scattered through the lamina propria --- but
the same pair \emph{on the epithelium} is a specific place.}

\subsection{What the strength threshold costs}

A new rule is kept only if its median lift reaches 1.3. Of \textbf{447} new attraction rules recurring in
$\geq5$ fields, \textbf{315} are discarded by it. For avoidance: 11 of 95.

\bio{Lift and reproducibility run opposite here, so a lift threshold preferentially cuts the rules that
repeat most --- lowering it is the change most likely to add real findings.}

\subsection{The same evidence for avoidance rules}

When two cell types never touch, lift is exactly 0 and any ratio explodes, so avoidance is never ranked
by measured gain here. The zero is real, though, and this is what it looks like in tissue.

\fig[0.92\textwidth]{avoids_fov_stronger.png}{A control colon field where Muscle and the two other types
never share a neighbourhood.}{fig:perfect-avoid}

\bio{Complete avoidance is usually a tissue boundary, not an interaction --- the method found the muscle
layer without being told it exists.}

Avoidance produces a significance win of its own, the same way attraction did.

\fig[0.92\textwidth]{avoids_fov_significance.png}{An avoidance rule whose FDR collapses once the third

\fig{gain_new_avoids.png}{New avoidance rules on the inverse-lift scale. Fibroblast is in ten of fifteen

\bio{A fibroblast-centred family of barriers is not an obvious guess --- and it is exactly what the atlas

\section{What the atlas reported, seen again in longer rules}
% =============================================================================

Checked against the \href{https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.adu6032}{spatial atlas of gastrointestinal

\subsection{The organised plasma-cell neighbourhood disappears}
\label{sec:zonation}

\atlas{We uncovered zonation of immune cells in the gut, in which CD4 T cells, plasma cells, and
macrophages were spatially organized from the crypts to the tip of the villus \ldots{} This recurrent
organization between individuals raises questions regarding the mechanisms that drive it, as well as its
functional implications in healthy gut homeostasis.}

\atlas{Homeostatic immune organization was disrupted, with a prominent reduction in immunoglobulin
A--secreting plasma cells.}

\note{Why a longer rule is the right test.}{The atlas measured zonation one cell type at a time, as a
distance from the crypt base. That says each type has a preferred height --- not that the types are still
next to each other. A three-cell rule asks exactly that.}

These are the colon attraction rules that change most between stages. What is striking is that they are
not twelve different findings --- they are one neighbourhood, and it reaches zero.

\fig{all_clin_attracts_colon_overview.png}{Colon attraction rules with the largest stage

\subsection{Goblet cells stop excluding immune cells}
\label{sec:goblet}

\atlas{In a subset of colon samples, we observed a loss of mucin expression in the epithelium, indicating
potential damage to goblet cells or impaired mucin production.}

\atlas{The lamina propria was enriched with plasma cells, macrophages, and CD4 T cells, whereas CD8 T and
double-negative T cells \ldots{} were the predominant populations inside the epithelium \ldots{}
Intraepithelial cells were primarily found in the duodenum and were very scarce in the colon.}

\note{Why a longer rule is the right test.}{Together those say the healthy colon keeps immune cells out of
the goblet-rich epithelium. That is a spatial exclusion, and an avoidance rule measures exclusion
directly. The atlas measured mucin loss as a stain intensity; the rule measures its consequence.}

The twelve colon avoidance rules that change most. Every single one begins with Goblet --- and the screen
was never told to look at goblet cells.

\fig{all_clin_avoids_colon_overview.png}{Colon avoidance rules with the largest stage change; all twelve
fall from control to severe.}{fig:overview-avo-colon-all}

\bio{Goblet exclusion emerged on its own from a ranking by stage change --- the mucin loss the atlas
stains for, seen as the separation it maintains.}

The clearest single rule, with its cell-abundance control alongside. This is the case Section~\ref{sec:zonation}
could not make.

\fig[0.92\textwidth]{all_clin_avoids_colon_rule_goblet_cd8t_plasma.png}{Goblet against CD8 T cells and
Plasma: 92\% of control fields, 15\% of severe, while the best pair stays at 81--100\%
throughout.}{fig:goblet-cd8-plasma}

\bio{Goblet cells are still at three quarters of their control share in mild disease, yet the exclusion
collapses from 92\% of fields to 6\% --- the cells are there and have stopped being kept apart.}

What that looks like in tissue: ordered bands in control, a fragmented compartment with immune cells
scattered through it in disease.

\fig[0.72\textwidth]{all_clin_avoids_colon_fovs_goblet_cd8t_plasma.png}{One field per
stage.}{fig:goblet-cd8-plasma-fovs}

The same rule with a different T cell behaves identically, which says this is about the goblet
compartment rather than about one immune cell type.

\section*{Limits}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}\tightlist
\item Every field result is tested. Every stage percentage and patient median is descriptive. No
patient-level test is run, although 71 patients are available for one.
\item Rules are selected by ranking across roughly 214{,}000 candidates, with no correction for that
selection.
\item For a new rule, ``gain'' is a floor set by the pair-mining threshold, not a measured ratio.
\item Lift is unstable at low support, and here the most recurrent rules carry the lowest lifts. The 1.3
threshold discards 315 of 447 recurrent new attraction rules.
\item Support gating is asymmetric by design: an attraction rule at confidence $\geq0.9$ is admitted at
half the usual support. Avoidance is gated on \emph{expected} meetings, which is why a rule can
legitimately report support 0.
\item How much muscularis a biopsy caught differs by stage, in opposite directions in the two organs. Any
result naming Muscle mixes biology with what the biopsy included. The fix --- repeating those figures on
muscularis-sampling fields only --- is not yet done.
\item Cell types are labels, not states. A rule naming Macrophage cannot distinguish macrophage
populations.
\item Cells being near each other is not evidence that one causes the other. An avoidance rule is very
often a tissue boundary rather than an interaction.
\end{itemize}
