\textbf{Muscle issue:} how much muscularis a biopsy caught differs by stage, and in
opposite directions in the two organs - it rises with stage in the duodenum and falls in
the colon. Any result involving Muscle therefore mixes biology with what the biopsy
happened to include, so every figure below that touches it is shown twice: all FOVs, then
only those that sampled the muscularis. The restricted version drops a few patients from
each severe group, so it is a check on the others rather than a replacement.

\textbf{Rules of a cell type with itself:} a rule may name the same cell type twice ---
\emph{Muscle beside Muscle}. These are \textbf{kept everywhere in this note}, and each one
lands on the diagonal of its grid. Every subtitle says whether they are in or out.
Section~4 is about what they show; Section~2 is the one place where keeping them changes
what a reader should conclude, so it draws that figure both ways.

\textbf{Counting a rule fairly:} a rule is counted only in an FOV that could have carried
it - one holding enough of both of its cell types to mine. Every fraction under a dot panel
is out of those FOVs, and every ring on a grid is the same thing drawn as a circle.

\section{Claim 1 - the direction of a rule is anatomical}

\textit{A $\rightarrow$ B} and \textit{B $\rightarrow$ A} are different measurements here,
and most mined rules fire one way round only. This is not an artefact of the thresholds: a
rule and its mirror have genuinely different lift, because a neighbourhood is centred on a
cell, so \emph{epithelium beside a Paneth cell} and \emph{a Paneth cell beside epithelium}
count different things.

\textbf{In one image, two squares are filled and their mirrors are empty.} Paneth cells have
epithelium beside them; smooth muscle has endothelium beside it. Neither reverse rule was
mined --- which is what the anatomy predicts, since Paneth cells are a fraction of a percent
of the tissue and almost no epithelial cell has one nearby.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{summary_downloads/f1_one_fov_control.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Reading the grid:} each \emph{row} is a cell type taken as the centre
    of a neighbourhood; each \emph{column} is a cell type found within $25\,\mu$m of it.
    A dot means that rule was mined, and \textbf{a heavy black outline means the mirror rule
    was not} --- it fired one way round only. A dot on the dashed diagonal is a cell type
    beside its own kind; it is its own mirror, so it is never outlined. Colour is log2 lift,
    so twice as likely and half as likely sit equally far from white. Dot size is

    \label{fig:fov1}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Across the cohort the asymmetry holds.} Paneth $\rightarrow$ Epithelial fires in
most of the control FOVs that could carry it and its mirror in none of them; SMV
$\rightarrow$ Endothelial sits above chance at every stage while its mirror is rare. The
panel below shows both pairs beside their mirrors, so the gap can be read directly.

\section{Claim 2 - the rules reconstruct the layers of the gut}

The gut is built in layers --- epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis --- and \textbf{nothing
about this was given to the mining}. No coordinates, no tissue regions, no anatomy: only
which cell types were found within $25\,\mu$m of each other. If the rules put that structure
back, they are measuring the tissue rather than the cell-type table.

\textbf{Folded into lineages, the grid shows the layering.} The diagonal is warm --- a layer
sits with itself --- and the epithelial row is cold against everything outside the
epithelium. Epithelium against muscle is the strongest avoidance anywhere in the data.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{summary_downloads/f4a_layers_one.pdf}
    \caption{Healthy colon, the cell types folded into their lineages; Muscle and Brunner
    glands keep their own row and column, forming a lineage with nothing else. A box
    averages the log2 lift of every rule inside it, \textbf{weighted by how many FOVs each
    rule appears in} --- otherwise a rule seen in one FOV would count as much as one seen in
    nearly all of them.}
    \label{fig:layers1}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The same picture repeats in both organs and at every stage,} and this is the
stereotypic organisation Azulay \textit{et al.} describe for healthy gut, recovered from
rules alone. That it barely moves with disease is the second half of the result rather than
a disappointment: a method that invented structure would drift, and this one does not, so
the changes it does report are worth taking seriously. Claim~3 is where they are.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{summary_downloads/f4b_layers_all.pdf}
    \caption{All six organ $\times$ stage groups, folded as in Fig.~\ref{fig:layers1}.}
    \label{fig:layers}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The warm diagonal is a layer sitting with itself, not only a cell type sitting with
itself.} A rule naming one cell type twice lands on the diagonal, so a warm
\emph{epithelial} box could in principle be nothing but Goblet beside Goblet. Dropping every
such rule cools the diagonal a little and leaves every box off it untouched, since no such
rule ever lands there. The layering is the stronger of the two statements.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{summary_downloads/f4d_layers_all_noself.pdf}
    \caption{Fig.~\ref{fig:layers} with every rule of a cell type with itself dropped. The
    Muscle and Brunner gland boxes on the diagonal empty out, because those two lineages
    hold one cell type each and had nothing but their own rule in them.}
    \label{fig:layersnoself}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Dropping the FOVs with no muscularis changes nothing but the Muscle row.} The
diagonal, the epithelial row and the stromal block are the same picture, so the layering is
not an artefact of which biopsies caught muscle.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{summary_downloads/f4c_layers_muscularis.pdf}
    \caption{Fig.~\ref{fig:layers} restricted to FOVs that sampled the muscularis.}
    \label{fig:layersmusc}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Unfolded, each pair sits at the same height in every stage --- the boundary does not
loosen.} Tested one rule at a time, with a Mann-Whitney on log2 lift and Benjamini-Hochberg
correction, almost no across-layer rule moves toward chance between control and severe.
Several tighten instead. The paper's ``less organised'' is about villus and crypt
\emph{shape}; a rule only asks which cell types end up within $25\,\mu$m of each other, and
a crypt can lose its shape while its cells keep their neighbours.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{summary_downloads/f5_layer_pairs.pdf}
    \caption{Colon, all FOVs. Three pairs across the layer boundary, drawn as in
    Fig.~\ref{fig:dir}.}
    \label{fig:layerpairs}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Epithelial $\rightarrow$ Muscle is the one pair the sampling could have invented,
and it survives.} On muscularis FOVs only it stays far below chance at every stage; the
other two pairs are unaffected, as they involve no muscle.

\section{Claim 3 - a rule is not its two cell types, counted}

Everything so far could still be answered with a cell-type table: if a rule only fired where
its two cell types happened to be common, the method would be an expensive way of counting.
Lift is built not to be --- it divides by how common each cell type is, so a rule away from
chance is already a claim about arrangement. This section tests that directly, and it is
the claim the method stands on.

\textbf{How strong a rule is has nothing to do with how common its cells are.} In healthy
tissue the two are uncorrelated (Spearman $\rho = 0.03$): a rule between two abundant cell
types is no stronger than one between two rare ones. Whatever the grid is measuring, it is
not the cell-type table read twice.

\textbf{And the two can move in opposite directions.} A handful of rules change strength
between control and severe \emph{against} the direction both of their cell types went ---
the cells go one way and the arrangement goes the other. They are named in red on the right
of the figure: Paneth and Goblet against epithelium and Plasma\_CD38 against Plasma all
\emph{tighten} while both their cell types thin out, and SMV beside SMV \emph{loosens} while
those cells become more common.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{summary_downloads/f13_dissociation.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left:} each dot is a cell pair in healthy tissue --- how common its two
    cell types are, against how strong the rule between them is. The cloud is flat.
    \textbf{Right:} how far each moved between control and severe. A dot in the upper left
    or lower right moved against its own cells; those are drawn in red and named.}
    \label{fig:dissoc}
\end{figure}

This is the part counting cannot reach. A cell-type table says \emph{Paneth cells are
disappearing}. The rule says \emph{the Paneth cells still there are packed against
epithelium more tightly than they were in health} --- a statement about what the surviving
tissue looks like, not about how much of it is left. Only the second needs spatial data.

\textbf{One diseased image already shows the composition changing too.} Against
Fig.~\ref{fig:fov1}, the Paneth row has gone and an Endocrine row has appeared.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{summary_downloads/f6_one_fov_severe.pdf}
    \caption{One severe duodenal FOV, drawn as Fig.~\ref{fig:fov1}.}
    \label{fig:fov2}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The paper's compositional findings are visible here too, and they are the weaker
half.} Paneth $\rightarrow$ Epithelial thins out with disease; Endocrine $\rightarrow$
Epithelial cannot be measured in healthy duodenum at all, because no control FOV holds
enough endocrine cells, and appears once disease sets in. These track the Paneth loss and
endocrine gain the paper reports. They confirm the rule table against a known result; they
do not add to it, and on their own they would not be worth the machinery.

\textbf{The Paneth rule is sharper against the pathological score than the clinical one,}
falling further in severe disease when the groups are cut by pathology. The endocrine rule
rises either way. The Lerner pathological score is the histological readout while the
Glucksberg clinical score grades symptoms --- the paper notes the two disagree in six of its
patients --- so a rule tracking the tissue score more closely is a small point in the
method's favour.

\section{Claim 4 - a cell type sits with its own kind}

A fair share of the mined rules name the same cell type twice, and for some of those the
answer is known before the mining runs: muscle is a continuous band, and Paneth cells sit
packed at the base of a crypt. Both should sit with their own kind, strongly. Both do, which makes
this the cheapest check in the note --- a method that got it wrong would be broken.

\textbf{Not one cell type avoids its own kind.} Every cell type carrying such a rule sits
above chance. The strongest are the ones the anatomy names --- Paneth, muscle, endocrine
cells, fibrotic epithelium, Brunner glands --- and the weakest are the ones spread thinly
through the lamina propria, macrophages and fibroblasts and T cells, which is where cells
that fill the tissue evenly belong.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{summary_downloads/f11_self_rules.pdf}
    \caption{Every FOV carrying a rule of a cell type with itself, both organs and every
    stage pooled, drawn as in Fig.~\ref{fig:dir}. The number after a name is how many FOVs
    carry the rule, and the names are coloured by cell group as on the grids above.}
    \label{fig:self}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Three cell types carry no such rule anywhere, and one of them is Epithelial} --- the
most common cell type in the data. That is the abundance handicap of the next section seen
from the inside: a cell type in nearly every neighbourhood cannot clear a lift threshold,
not even beside itself. The other two, APC and NK, sit at the opposite end, among the
rarest.

\textbf{Two of these rules follow the disease, and two make good controls.} In the colon,
Plasma beside Plasma is common in healthy tissue and rare in severe, which is the plasma
cell loss the paper reports; in the duodenum, Paneth beside Paneth thins the same way. The
controls stay put: Goblet beside Goblet holds throughout the colon, and Muscle beside Muscle
through the duodenum, which is what a band of muscle should do whether or not the patient is
ill. As in Claim~3, the count is what carries the signal --- the heights barely move, except
for Plasma, which climbs: the plasma cells still there sit closer together than they did in
health.

\section{What the method cannot answer}

\textbf{The grid is sparse.} A median FOV yields a few dozen rules out of nearly a thousand
possible pairs, so an untrimmed grid is almost entirely empty. Trimming to the cell types
actually used makes one grid far easier to read; the shared axis is kept only where grids
must be compared.

\textbf{Abundant cell types are handicapped.} A cell type in nearly every neighbourhood has a
lift near chance by construction and cannot clear the threshold. Epithelial cells are the
case in point: the most common type in the data, and not one rule with themselves anywhere
in the cohort. This also puts the paper's intraepithelial-lymphocyte result out of reach ---
the signs are right at every stage, CD8T $\rightarrow$ Epithelial above chance and CD4T
$\rightarrow$ Epithelial below it, but the rules clear threshold in too few FOVs to test.

\textbf{One FOV is coarser than one crypt.} The paper's local-microenvironment result (their
Fig.~6) is measured in a ring around each crypt, where an infiltrated crypt can sit beside an
untouched one. A rule is mined over a whole FOV, which averages the two together. Within-FOV
patchiness would need mining at crypt level --- the natural next step.
